Exponent laws explained (Grade 10–11 math)

A plain-English guide for Ontario students and parents: every exponent law with an example, where each one shows up in high school math, and the mistakes that cost the most marks.

QueensCoaching parent guide · Updated November 2026

The short answerExponent laws are shortcuts for working with powers. When you multiply powers with the same base you add the exponents, when you divide you subtract them, and when you raise a power to a power you multiply them. Zero, negative and fractional exponents follow from these same rules, and they matter in every Ontario math course from Grade 9 to Grade 12.

What is an exponent?

A power has a base and an exponent. In 25, the base is 2 and the exponent is 5, which means 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 32. The exponent tells you how many times the base is used as a factor.

Every exponent law comes from that one idea. If your teen ever forgets a law, they can write the powers out the long way and see the pattern again. That habit alone fixes many mistakes.

Where do exponent laws show up in Ontario math?

  • Grade 9 (MTH1W): the Number strand introduces powers and the patterns behind the exponent laws.
  • Grade 10 (MPM2D): exponent laws are used constantly when expanding, simplifying and factoring expressions such as 3x2(2x − 5) in the quadratics unit.
  • Grade 11 (MCR3U): the Exponential Functions strand extends the laws to zero, negative and rational exponents, then uses them for exponential growth and decay.
  • Grade 12 (MHF4U and MCV4U): logarithms are built directly on exponent laws, and the power rule for derivatives in Calculus depends on rewriting roots and fractions as exponents.
  • Science: scientific notation in Chemistry and Physics, such as (3.0 × 108) × (2.0 × 10−3) = 6.0 × 105, is exponent laws in action.

This is why a shaky Grade 9 or 10 skill often shows up as a low mark in Grade 11 Functions. See our MCR3U course page for how we coach that unit.

Why are negative and zero exponents confusing?

Most students accept the product and quotient laws quickly. Zero and negative exponents feel strange because you cannot "use 2 as a factor −3 times". The quotient law explains them:

  • 53 ÷ 53 = 1, and the quotient law gives 53−3 = 50. So 50 must equal 1.
  • 52 ÷ 55 = 1/53, and the quotient law gives 52−5 = 5−3. So 5−3 = 1/53 = 1/125.

A negative exponent means "take the reciprocal". It never makes the answer negative on its own.

The most common exponent mistakes

  • Multiplying exponents instead of adding: x3 × x4 is x7, not x12.
  • Forgetting the coefficient: (2x3)2 is 4x6, not 2x6. The 2 is squared too.
  • Treating a negative exponent as a negative number: 2−3 is 1/8, not −8.
  • Mixing up −32 and (−3)2: −32 = −9, but (−3)2 = 9. Brackets decide what is squared.
  • Spreading an exponent over addition: (a + b)2 is a2 + 2ab + b2, not a2 + b2. Exponent laws work over multiplication, not addition.
  • Combining different bases: 23 × 32 is not 65.

How to master exponent laws

  • Learn the "why", not just the rule. Writing x3 × x4 out as seven x's makes the product law obvious and easy to remember.
  • Do one step at a time. Simplify coefficients first, then each variable, then deal with negative exponents last.
  • Turn roots into exponents. In Grade 11 and 12, writing √x as x1/2 makes most questions easier.
  • Practise mixed questions. Tests mix all the laws together, so practice should too.
  • Check with numbers. If unsure, test the rule with small numbers, like 22 × 23 = 4 × 8 = 32 = 25.

What parents can do

You do not need to remember the rules to help:

  • Ask your teen to explain why x0 = 1. If they can explain it, they understand it; if not, that is a gap worth fixing.
  • Encourage ten minutes of mixed practice a few times a week rather than one long session before a test.
  • Watch for exponent mistakes in Grade 10 and 11 tests. They often look like "careless errors" but are really a missing foundation.

Questions families ask

Which grade learns exponent laws in Ontario?

Powers and the patterns behind the exponent laws start in Grade 9 Math (MTH1W). They are used throughout Grade 10 (MPM2D) and extended to negative and rational exponents in Grade 11 Functions (MCR3U).

Why does anything to the power of zero equal 1?

Because dividing a power by itself gives 1, and the quotient law gives an exponent of zero. For example, 4 cubed divided by 4 cubed is 1, and it is also 4 to the power of 0. The rule applies to any non-zero base.

Do exponent laws matter in Grade 12?

Yes. Logarithms in MHF4U are built on exponent laws, and the power rule for derivatives in MCV4U depends on writing roots and fractions as exponents.
